<p class="art-lede-p">
  <span class="dropcap">I</span>t started with a shelf of Mac Minis running hot in a corner of the
  office and a question that would not go away: if a meaningful share of our intellectual output now
  flows through language models, why do we still treat those models like a subscription someone else
  manages for us? We would never run a factory on a single supplier with no backup line. We would
  never power a hospital from one grid with no fallback. And yet there we were — an entire
  development organisation, thinking through a single pipe, priced by someone else, throttled by
  someone else, available exactly when someone else's capacity allowed.
</p>

<p>
  So over the last weeks we changed how we develop at Plan.Net Studios. The result is what we call
  the <strong>Agent Fleet</strong> — a standing crew of AI agents that works on our codebase around
  the clock — and the architecture that emerged is hybrid at every single layer. Not hybrid as a
  hedge. Hybrid as a decision about who controls the conditions of our own thinking.
</p>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">What actually runs</h2>

<p>
  The setup, concretely: <strong>Claude Code</strong> is our primary coding harness on the Claude
  models, for the work where frontier quality earns its price. <strong>OpenClaw</strong> is the
  assistant and orchestration harness, running open source models — currently Nemotron 3 Super —
  with <strong>OpenShell</strong> supplying the guardrails. A stack of Mac Minis works on the
  codebase 24/7 without interruption: end-to-end testing, refactoring, bug-fixing — the maintenance
  work humans postpone and agents never tire of. A <strong>DGX Spark</strong> sits on-premise,
  running the open source models as the orchestration and support layer, and as overflow capacity
  for those moments when the frontier cloud slows down or hits its limits — which is happening more
  often now than any roadmap slide admits.
</p>

<p>
  Nobody herds this fleet task by task. A central <strong>Nexus agent</strong> makes it addressable —
  from Telegram, from email, and from <strong>Sōkosumi</strong>, where an agent is commissioned the
  way you would brief a colleague. The fleet coordinates itself through GitHub: issues, branches,
  pull requests, review. It turns out the infrastructure software teams already trusted to carry
  their collaboration carries agent collaboration just as well. Every diff, every decision lands
  where a human can inspect it. That is not a nice-to-have. That is the whole difference between a
  fleet you command and a fleet you merely hope about.
</p>

<blockquote class="art-quote">
  <p class="serif">
    When thinking becomes a process, it acquires a supply chain — and a supply chain is only as
    sovereign as its second option.
  </p>
</blockquote>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">The operator's questions</h2>

<p>
  The moment you frame it that way, the right questions stop being chat-subscription questions and
  become operator questions. Constant token throughput. Cost per token generated. Uptime of token
  production. The ratio of humans to tokens produced. There is nothing exotic on that list — it is
  the same sheet any plant manager, grid operator, or data-centre lead has kept for a century. What
  is new is only what sits at the end of the line: the act of thinking itself.
</p>

<p>
  What made this architecture possible <em>now</em> is that open source models crossed a line.
  Nemotron 3 Super, the current Qwen generation, their peers — they produce tokens at a completely
  different price point, at respectable speed, and at a quality level that lets you hand them
  production-relevant work instead of demos. That changes the math. Frontier models stop being the
  only engine and become the premium layer — used where their quality pays for itself — while open
  source capacity carries the volume. Cost stops being a reason to think less. Dependency on any
  one provider stops being a fact on the floor.
</p>

<p>
  A confession: I ignored OpenClaw for work purposes for a long time. What moved me was not a
  benchmark, it was <strong>guardrails</strong> — OpenShell gives you enough control over what an
  autonomous agent may and may not touch that letting it into your infrastructure stops being an
  act of faith. I keep arguing that trust is an architecture, not a feeling. Adoption obeys the same
  law: you hand work to what you can inspect and contain.
</p>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">A proving ground, not an end state</h2>

<p>
  To be clear about what the Minis and the Spark are: a start. A way to prove the concept, and to
  test a question I find genuinely interesting — whether a developer's environment should sit
  close, on-premise, next to the MacBook, with no hard dependency on a single model provider. It
  has real pros and real cons; anyone claiming the answer is obvious is selling something. At some
  point the pattern moves into a VPC of our own. Still evaluating, still evolving — but the
  direction is set.
</p>

<p>
  And the direction rhymes with something I have argued before: sovereignty begins with where the
  server sits. The Agent Fleet is that thesis built in hardware. The machines are on the desk. The
  orchestration runs through channels we own. The open source capacity belongs to whoever pays the
  electricity. None of this is secrecy — we use public clouds and frontier models gladly, wherever
  they are the right tool. It is about staying an organisation that <em>could</em> keep thinking if
  any one of them became unavailable, expensive, or merely inconvenient.
</p>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">The pattern underneath</h2>

<p>
  What surprised me is how far the pattern generalises. Take any layer of the agentic stack and ask
  one question: what is the second option, and what keeps it warm? Humans and agents — the human
  layer is not a fallback, it is the seat of intent. Cloud and on-premise — each class of task runs
  where it runs best, and neither can starve the other. Frontier and open source — premium where
  quality pays, open where volume and cost discipline matter. Coding harness and orchestration
  harness — one owns the work, the other owns the flow.
</p>

<p>
  In every one of those pairings, hybrid is not the average of the two sides. It is a standing
  capability to choose — per task, on purpose. That is also where this connects to everything else
  I build. The Drift is what happens when convenience quietly narrows your options until one path
  remains and it belongs to somebody else. Hybrid is the Drift in reverse: options deliberately
  kept wide, at real cost in complexity, so that choosing remains ours. It is more work than going
  all-in on one vendor. It is also, I have come to believe, the only stance that lets you scale
  into an agentic economy without ending up a tenant in it.
</p>

<p>
  So yes — we are going all-in on hybrid, in every single aspect of AI agents. Not because the two
  sides of any pair are equally good. Because the ability to switch between them is the last
  capability you can afford to lose.
</p>
