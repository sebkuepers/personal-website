<p class="art-lede-p">
  <span class="dropcap">W</span>e are going all-in on hybrid, in every layer of how we build with AI
  agents. The word sounds like a hedge — half one thing, half another, the coward's middle. But that
  is not what hybrid is. Done properly, hybrid is not a compromise between two options. It is the
  deliberate refusal to let <em>any single option</em> become a dependency. It is sovereignty applied
  to architecture: every layer of the stack gets a counterpart, so that no vendor, no cloud, no model
  family, and no single machine holds the only key to your ability to think and ship.
</p>

<p>
  The immediate occasion is practical. Over the last weeks we rebuilt the way we develop at Plan.Net
  Studios around what we call the <strong>Agent Fleet</strong> — a standing crew of AI agents that
  works on our codebase around the clock. The fleet is deliberately hybrid at every joint:
  <strong>humans combined with agents</strong>, <strong>cloud combined with on-premise
  infrastructure</strong>, <strong>premium frontier models combined with open source models</strong>,
  <strong>a coding harness combined with an orchestration harness</strong>. One layer of each kind,
  always. Not because we could not pick a winner, but because a stack with only one of anything is a
  stack with a single point of failure — and in an agentic workflow, the failure is not an outage.
  It is a stoppage of thinking.
</p>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">The fleet, concretely</h2>

<p>
  In practice it looks like this. <strong>Claude Code</strong> is our primary coding harness, running
  the Claude models for the work where frontier quality matters most. <strong>OpenClaw</strong> is
  the assistant and orchestration harness, running open source models — currently Nemotron 3 Super —
  with OpenShell providing the guardrails that make an autonomous agent in your infrastructure
  something you can actually live with. A stack of Mac Minis works on the codebase 24/7 without
  interruption: end-to-end tests, refactoring, bug-fixing, the tireless maintenance work that humans
  procrastinate on and agents never do. A <strong>DGX Spark</strong> runs the open source models
  locally — orchestration and support layer, and overflow capacity for the moments when the frontier
  cloud slows down or hits capacity, which is happening more often than anyone's marketing would
  suggest.
</p>

<p>
  The humans do not babysit this fleet one task at a time. A central <strong>Nexus agent</strong> is
  how the fleet becomes addressable — through Telegram, through email, and through
  <strong>Sōkosumi</strong>, our own agentic services marketplace, where an agent is commissioned
  the way you would brief a colleague. And the fleet coordinates itself through GitHub: issues,
  branches, pull requests, review. The infrastructure of software collaboration turned out to be
  the natural nervous system for agent collaboration too. Every artefact of the work — every
  decision, every diff — lands where humans can inspect it.
</p>

<blockquote class="art-quote">
  <p class="serif">
    When your intellectual value creation depends on tokens, tokens are no longer a purchase. They
    are infrastructure — and infrastructure is judged on throughput, cost, uptime, and who
    controls it.
  </p>
</blockquote>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">Token economics is infrastructure thinking</h2>

<p>
  Something shifts in how you think the moment more of your actual intellectual output flows through
  a model. You stop shopping for a chat subscription and start thinking like an operator: constant
  token throughput, cost per token generated, uptime of token production, and the ratio of humans
  to tokens produced. None of that is exotic. It is the same logic anyone who ever ran a factory, a
  power grid, or a data centre would recognise immediately. What is new is only that the production
  line is now the act of thinking.
</p>

<p>
  This is where open source models have quietly crossed a line. Models like Nemotron 3 Super or the
  current Qwen generation produce tokens at a fundamentally different price point, at good speed —
  and, more importantly, at a level of quality that lets them take over production-relevant tasks
  rather than toy ones. That changes the shape of a sensible architecture. Premium frontier models
  are no longer the only engine; they become the premium layer, reserved for the work that needs
  them, while open source capacity carries the load that does not. Dependency on any single
  provider drops, resilience rises, and cost stops being a reason to think less.
</p>

<p>
  I will admit I ignored OpenClaw for work purposes for a long time. What changed my mind was
  guardrails — OpenShell and the patterns around it give you enough control over what an autonomous
  agent can and cannot touch that incorporating it stops being an act of faith. Trust, as I keep
  arguing, is an architecture, not a feeling. The same is true for adoption: you adopt what you can
  inspect and contain.
</p>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">Where the server sits — again</h2>

<p>
  Running all of this on a DGX Spark and a shelf of Mac Minis is obviously not the end state. It is
  a proving ground: a way to validate the concept, and to evaluate a genuinely interesting question —
  whether a developer's dev environment should sit close, on-premise, next to the MacBook rather than
  in someone else's cloud, without a hard dependency on any single model provider. It has pros and
  cons, and anyone who tells you it is obviously one or the other is selling something. At some point
  the right move is to carry the pattern into a VPC of one's own. Still evaluating, still evolving —
  but the direction is set.
</p>

<p>
  I have written before that sovereignty begins with where the server sits. The Agent Fleet is that
  thesis, expressed in hardware instead of argument. The machines are on the desk. The orchestration
  runs through channels we own. The open source capacity belongs to whoever owns the electricity.
  None of this is about secrecy — we build on public clouds and frontier models gladly. It is about
  remaining an organisation that <em>could</em> keep thinking if any one of them became unavailable,
  expensive, or merely inconvenient.
</p>

<h2 class="serif art-h2">Hybrid as a general principle</h2>

<p>
  What surprised me is how far the pattern generalises. Pick any layer of the agentic stack and ask:
  what is the second option, and what keeps it warm? Humans and agents — the human layer is not a
  fallback, it is the part that holds intent. Cloud and on-premise — each class of task runs where it
  runs best, and neither is a single point of failure. Frontier and open source — premium where
  quality pays for itself, open where volume and cost discipline matter. Coding harness and
  orchestration harness — one owns the work, the other owns the flow. In every pairing, hybrid is
  not the average of the two. It is a standing capability to choose, per task, on purpose.
</p>

<p>
  That is the connection to everything else I build. The Drift happens when convenience quietly
  narrows your options until there is only one path left and it belongs to someone else. A hybrid
  architecture is the Drift, reversed: a deliberate widening of options, maintained at some real
  cost in complexity, precisely so that choosing remains possible. It is more work than going
  all-in on one vendor. It is also, I have come to believe, the only stance that scales into an
  agentic economy without turning you into a tenant of it.
</p>

<p>
  Go hybrid — in every single aspect. Not because the options are equally good, but because the
  ability to switch between them is the capability you can least afford to lose.
</p>
